## Abstract 

Food segmentation is essential for applications such as intelligent catering, dietary assessment, and recommendation. However, exist-ing benchmarks fail to capture the complexity of real-world dining scenes. The challenges of dense inter-dish overlap, fine-grained class similarity, and extreme long-tail class distributions exceed the fidelity of current datasets. To fill this gap, we introduce Dish-Seg24k , a large-scale dish-level segmentation benchmark with 24,096 images, 112,281 instances, and 278 fine-grained categories in real-world dining environments. Based on DishSeg24k, we further propose Food Expert-Adaptive Segmentation Transformers (FEAST) to address these challenges. FEAST models query-based decoding as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), where each de-coder layer update is treated as a sequential decision step that explores uncertainty along dish boundaries. We further redesign the decoder with a reinforcement learning (RL)-guided Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) module, in which a dual-critic decoupled opti-mization scheme separates task-oriented query refinement from structure-aware expert routing. ## 1 Introduction 

Food segmentation, which assigns a category label to every food-relevant region in an image, is important for intelligent catering systems in restaurants and canteens. These systems price meals on a per-dish basis, requiring each dish in a dining image to be accurately recognized. Beyond that, precise dish-level masks also enable volume estimation of individual food items [ 3 ], which in turn supports dietary assessment and personalized nutritional rec-ommendation [ 1]. Unlike general-purpose object segmentation, food segmentation must resolve the compositional complexity of real-world dining scenes. Dishes are not isolated rigid objects, but visually entangled ensembles of mixed ingredients served in shared, cluttered spaces [25, 37]. arXiv:2607.23070v1 [cs.CV] 25 Jul 2026 Conference’17, July 2017, Washington, DC, USA Yilin Wang, Haochen Shi, Guanyu Chen, Weiqing Min, Jinkai Zheng, Chenggang Yan, and Shuqiang Jiang

This compositional nature causes three distinct challenges. (i) Dense inter-dish overlap. Multiple dishes are often served in shared con-tainers, compartment trays, or closely packed plates. The resulting spatial overlap and adjacency make it difficult to delineate individ-ual dish masks. For example, several stir-fried dishes may share the same tray section with heavily overlapping regions (e.g., row 3, col. 2 in Fig. 2). (ii) Fine-grained class similarity. Different dish classes often share common ingredients. For instance, both “scrambled eggs with tomato” and “stir-fried beef with tomato” contain tomato, pro-ducing locally indistinguishable visual features. Even when spatial boundaries are clear, the model must determine which class a region belongs to from limited local evidence. (iii) Long-tail class distribu-tion. The natural consumption frequency of dishes follows a severe long-tail pattern. Common staples (e.g., Staple-Rice ) dominate the training set, while rare regional specialties may have few instances. This skewed distribution biases models toward frequent classes. Existing methods across multiple paradigms also fall short. CNN-based methods [ 6] lack global context for dense overlap. Multi-modal pre-training [ 37 ], self-supervised learning [ 21 ], and open-vocabulary approaches [ 39 ] improve representations yet remain too coarse for pixel-level boundary disambiguation. Foundation-model adaptations [ 19 ] introduce segmentation capability but lack category-level discrimination under class imbalance. Query-based frameworks such as Mask2Former [ 7] rely on de-terministic decoding, which suppresses hypothesis diversity and limits robustness in ambiguous food scenes. This limitation is exac-erbated by their use of a single static Feedforward Network (FFN), whose fixed capacity cannot adequately model heterogeneous dish textures. A natural remedy is to introduce MoE routing, but under long-tailed data distributions, naive MoE training often leads to expert collapse [9, 38]. Existing benchmarks are limited to clearly separated single-dish layouts [ 30 ] or ingredient-level annotations [ 37 ], failing to capture the complexity of real-world dining scenes. To address this gap, we first introduce DishSeg24k , a large-scale dish-level segmentation benchmark constructed from real-world dining scenes, including 24,096 images, 112,281 instances, and 278 fine-grained categories. The dataset was established through multiple rounds of iterative annotation and rigorous multi-stage quality control, ensuring ac-curate segmentation boundaries and high annotation consistency across complex dining scenes. Fig. 2 illustrates the diversity within DishSeg24k. Each row corresponds to a distinct serving style (e.g., single plates, compartment trays, multi-section metal trays), while columns progress from simple to complex segmentation scenarios, revealing how the challenges intensify as scene composition grows more complex. As shown in Fig. 1, DishSeg24k exhibits substantially denser inter-dish overlap and stronger fine-grained class similarity compared with UECFoodPixComplete [30] and FoodSeg103 [37]. Furthermore, we propose Food Expert-Adaptive Segmenta-tion Transformers (FEAST) , an RL-guided decoder designed for dish-level segmentation in complex dining scenes. FEAST reformu-lates query-based mask decoding as a sequential decision process, where the update at each decoder layer is treated as an action in a Markov Decision Process (MDP). This formulation enables object queries to explore multiple plausible semantic hypotheses before convergence, alleviating the rigid deterministic behavior that often fails under inter-dish overlap. To address boundary ambiguity from mixed ingredients, we replace the standard FFN with a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) decoder, in which query states are adaptively routed to specialized experts. To stabilize this routing under long-tailed data, we further introduce a dual-critic decoupled optimization scheme that separately supervises task-oriented mask refinement and structure-aware expert routing, encouraging expert specializa-tion while preventing expert collapse. Extensive experiments on DishSeg24k demonstrate the state-of-the-art performance of FEAST, with gains of +3.21% mIoU, +3.68% mDice, and +4.00% mAcc over previous methods. To further assess effectiveness, we evaluate FEAST on the FoodSeg103 [ 37 ]. Under the same ResNet-50 setting, FEAST achieves an additional +1.47% mIoU improvement over existing approaches. Our contributions are summarized as follows. (i) We introduce DishSeg24k, a large-scale dish-level food segmentation dataset, which is designed to capture dense overlap, fine-grained class simi-larity, and long-tail distributions in real-world dining scenes. (ii) We propose FEAST, which formulates query-based decoding as an RL-optimized MDP. It further introduces an MoE decoder with a dual-critic decoupled optimization scheme to improve both uncertainty-aware mask refinement and structure-aware expert routing. (iii) We conduct comprehensive experiments on DishSeg24k by evaluating FEAST against 13 cutting-edge segmentation methods. We further validate the effectiveness of FEAST on FoodSeg103.

Food Segmentation Methods. Methods applied to food seg-mentation can be broadly grouped into general-purpose architec-tures and food-specific designs. Among general-purpose methods, CNN-based approaches such as DeepLabV3+ [ 6] remain widely used baselines but are limited by fixed receptive fields. Per-pixel Transformer methods address this by introducing global context: SegFormer [ 42 ] combines a hierarchical encoder with a lightweight MLP decoder, and subsequent works further improve embedding efficiency [ 44 ] and contextual reasoning [ 29 ], and omni-scale fea-ture aggregation via state-space models (SegMAN [ 12 ]). A more fundamental shift is the mask-classification paradigm, where Mask-Former [ 8 ] reformulates segmentation as predicting class-labeled binary masks via learnable queries. Mask2Former [ 7 ] advances this with masked cross-attention and multi-scale query refinement, serving as the foundation for task-conditioned unification (One-Former [ 16 ]), state-space encoder integration (VMFormer [ 43 ]), and encoder-derived query initialization (FeedFormer [ 34 ]). However, across all these architectures, query updates follow deterministic trajectories to converge toward a single semantic hypothesis. It hinders hypothesis diversity in composition-entangled food scenes. Food-specific methods follow a clearer progression. Early efforts focus on representation enhancement: ReLeM [ 37 ] aligns visual features with recipe language embeddings to reduce intra-class variance, and FeaSC [ 21 ] improves discriminability through self-supervised contrastive pre-training. Subsequent efforts shift toward transferability and category scalability: FoodSAM [ 19 ] adapts SAM for zero-shot food segmentation, while OVFoodSeg [ 39 ] introduces open-vocabulary recognition via text–image alignment. More re-cently, FoodMask [ 27 ] moves toward unified multi-task modeling by jointly handling counting, segmentation, and recognition. Al-though these advances strengthen food-domain representation and category coverage, they still rely on deterministic query refinement and do not explicitly resolve the visual-semantic ambiguity caused by inter-dish overlap and inter-class similarity. FEAST addresses this limitation by casting query refinement as a RL-guided sto-chastic decision process with MoE routing for active hypothesis exploration.

## 3.1 Dataset Collection 

We build our dataset from two complementary sources. First, we collect additional 3,493 real-world dining images from publicly avail-able online sources. This subset intentionally focuses on Chinese dining scenarios, where food items are more frequently shared and visually entangled, resulting in complex mixtures, heavy occlusions, and ambiguous boundaries. Second, we adopt raw images from the ZSFood dataset [ 45 ], which contains 20,603 food images across 10 restaurant scenarios with 95,322 bounding boxes covering 291 classes. Notably, ZSFood includes both Western and Chinese-style dishes, providing diverse yet relatively structured dining scenes. We discard the original bounding-box annotations and perform complete pixel-level re-annotation with polygon masks under our unified hierarchical labeling protocol. All images undergo quality filtering to ensure sufficient visual clarity and contextual realism. The resulting dataset spans restaurants, canteens, and home envi-ronments, featuring varied dish arrangements from isolated plates to highly cluttered or shared containers. By combining structured scenes from ZSFood with highly complex Chinese dining cases, our dataset provides a more comprehensive and challenging benchmark for dish-level food segmentation. 

## 3.2 Annotation Protocol 

Label Taxonomy Design 1 We design a hierarchical taxonomy tailored for cuisines, by using a composition-based naming conven-tion: [Category]–[Ingredient]–[Optional Cooking Method] (e.g., MeatVeg-Beef-Potato). Dishes are organized into 6 major categories: Staple, Soup, Fruit, Vegetarian, Meat-Only, and Meat-Veg, and clas-sified into 278 dish-level labels defined by key ingredients. Cooking methods are included only when the same ingredient combina-tions are cooked visually distinctive (e.g., Steamed, Braised), with stir-frying is the default. Although our dataset focus on dish-level segmentation, additionally provided ingredient-level annotations

Annotation Workflow. We employ a multi-stage annotation process on the XAnyLabel [ 35 ] platform. First, six expert annotators spent two months on a pilot phase, labeling 2,000 samples to estab-lish boundary criteria and refine taxonomy definitions. Production then scaled to 15 annotators over two additional months. To balance efficiency and precision, we use AI-assisted labeling. Specifically, Annotators remodify masks to pixel-level accuracy based on initial masks that are generated by foundation models (e.g., SAM [ 17 ]). For cluttered scenes, each visually distinguishable dish receives an individual mask, while overlapping ingredients are assigned unified masks based on dominant dish identity to maintain semantic co-herence. Finally, to conduct quality control, a three-tier verification ensures reliability. (i) annotators self-check against guidelines. (ii) Quality Assurance teams sample 20% of weekly output, rejecting batches below 95% pixel-accuracy for re-annotation. (iii) domain experts conduct final reviews to correct category misclassifications and boundary inconsistencies.

## 4 FEAST Method 4.1 Overview 

Fig. 4(a) shows the overall architecture of FEAST, which consists of three primary components: a visual feature encoder, a pixel decoder, and a novel stochastic Transformer Decoder. Consequently, a set of 𝑁 learnable object queries is iteratively refined through 

𝐾 Transformer decoder layers. The refined queries are projected into class logits and binary masks via prediction heads, following the standard Mask2Former pipeline [ 7 ]. Our key departure is to reformulate the deterministic query update across decoder layers as a stochastic sequential decision process . At each stochastic policy de-coder layer 𝑘 , a MoE module replaces the standard FFN to produce specialized features (Fig. 4(b)), and a stochastic policy samples a continuous semantic shift as the query update (Fig. 4(c)). A dual-critic optimization scheme provides decoupled reward signals to separately guide mask refinement and expert routing (Fig. 4(d)).

## 5 Experiment 5.1 Experimental Settings 

Datasets. We evaluate FEAST on two benchmarks. (i) DishSeg24k comprises 24,096 images (19,365/4,731 train/test) with 112,281 pixel-level annotations spanning 278 dish categories from authentic Chi-nese dining scenes. It features a long-tail distribution, high instance density, and severe inter-dish overlap. (ii) We further evaluate FEAST on FoodSeg103 [37] for the effectiveness of our method. 

Evaluation Metrics. We report mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU), mean Dice coefficient (mDice), and mean class accuracy (mAcc) on the respective test sets. 

Implementation Details. FEAST is built on Detectron2 [ 40 ]with a ResNet-50 [ 14 ] and Swin-B [ 22 ] backbone pretrained on ImageNet [ 10 ]. We use AdamW (lr 1×10 −4, weight decay 0.05 , step decay). Training crops are 512 ×512 , while testing uses original resolution. The decoder has 𝐾 =9 Transformer layers with 𝑁 =100 

queries and 𝐸 𝑟 =4 routed experts with Top-2 soft routing. All models train for 80k iterations on 8 ×NVIDIA 3090 GPUs with batch size 16. The reference policy 𝜋 ref is the frozen pretrained Mask2Former decoder. Advantages are estimated via GAE [ 32 ]. All competing methods use official code with the same backbone and training protocol.

## 6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we study food segmentation in real-world dining scenes with severe ambiguity, overlap, and long-tail distributions. We introduce DishSeg24k to capture these challenges and pro-pose an RL-guided MoE decoding framework for adaptive mask refinement beyond deterministic query optimization. Beyond strong empirical performance, our results suggest that handling seman-tic entanglement requires not only stronger representations but also dynamic inference mechanisms. Furthermore, the fine-grained ingredient-level annotations and precise region masks in Dish-Seg24k provide a fundamental basis for downstream tasks such as portion and volume estimation, nutrient analysis, and dietary as-sessment, which critically rely on accurate segmentation. We hope this work encourages future research toward unified frameworks for comprehensive food understanding in real-world scenarios.
