# Circuit Fine-Tuning for Compute-Efficient Transformer Adaptation 

## Uri Z. Kialy, Gil Ben-Artzi 

School of Computer Science, Ariel University, Israel uri.kialy@msmail.ariel.ac.il, gilba@ariel.ac.il 

Abstract 

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) has become the de facto standard for adapting Vision Transformers (ViTs) to downstream tasks. While parameter count has been the dom-inant efficiency metric in PEFT, it does not imply com-pute efficiency : parameter-sparse methods can still incur full-model training cost per step, and typically need long sched-ules to reach peak accuracy. We introduce Circuit Fine-Tuning (CFT), a compute-efficient framework that uses circuit discovery—conventionally used to explain trained models— to select modules for fine-tuning before training. Whereas attribution is conventionally formulated against a trained task head, we formulate it against a near-zero-initialized probe head, which isolates the response of the backbone to the tar-get distribution rather than the preferences of a particular classifier. CFT then fine-tunes only the recovered subgraph. CFT needs no learning-rate warmup and reaches peak ac-curacy in ∼20 epochs on average—versus 44 –96 for strong PEFT baselines—yielding 2.3–6.6× fewer training FLOPs and up to 16 × less wall-clock time, while adding zero pa-rameters and no inference operations. Experiments across a standard visual transfer benchmark (VTAB-1k), hierarchical backbones (Swin), domain-shifted medical imaging (CBIS-DDSM), and a vision-language model (Gemma-3 on CUB-200) demonstrate the effectiveness of CFT. Code is available at https://github.com/UriKialy/CFT. 

## 1 Introduction 

The emergence of large-scale Vision Transformers (ViTs) (Dosovitskiy et al. 2021) has shifted the central chal-lenge in computer vision from architecture design to effi-cient adaptation. Full Fine-Tuning (FFT), updating all pa-rameters on a downstream task, remains the conceptual gold standard but becomes computationally expensive as models scale. This has driven the rapid growth of Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT), where the goal is to adapt a frozen back-bone by updating as few parameters as possible. The PEFT literature has converged on two dominant strategies. Additive and modulation methods ( e.g. , Houlsby et al. 2019; Jia et al. 2022; Hu et al. 2022; Lian et al. 2022) insert new learnable modules into the frozen backbone and apply adaptations uni-formly across all layers. Sensitivity-based methods ( e.g. , He et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024) instead select specific param-eters or modules to update. Both strategies target the same axis—parameter count. 

Parameter efficiency ̸ = compute efficiency. Parameter count is not a good proxy for training effort: it says nothing about how many epochs are needed, or how many FLOPs and how much wall-clock time a method needs to converge. Tun-ing 3% vs. 15% of parameters—a 5× difference—changes per-iteration cost by only ∼6% . The reason is that the cost of a training iteration is dominated by terms independent of the number of updated parameters. To first order, a sin-gle iteration costs ≈ (2+ α)/3 of full-training cost, where α

is the fraction of updated parameters: one forward and one activation-gradient pass are incurred regardless of α, and only the weight-gradient term scales with it (see Sec. 3.3). Consequently, the dominant factor in compute-efficient fine-tuning is not parameter count but convergence speed: the number of epochs required to reach peak accuracy. 

From PEFT to CEFT. We present a Compute-Efficient Fine-Tuning (CEFT) approach, whose objective is to im-prove the compute-to-accuracy trade-off. We define this trade-off as the relationship between downstream accuracy and the total compute needed to reach this accuracy, where total compute is cumulative training FLOPs and wall-clock time. 

Circuit Adaptation. What makes a pretrained model eas-ier to adapt? A model pretrained on a distribution Dpre should adapt more readily to a target distribution Dtgt that is close to Dpre than to an arbitrary one. Yet, given a fixed checkpoint 

fθ0 , we have no control over Dpre , so this observation is not directly actionable at the level of the whole model. Research in mechanistic interpretability (Olah et al. 2020; Elhage et al. 2021) has shown that Transformers implement sparse sub-graphs, or circuits, but these are typically identified in a frozen model to explain existing behavior. Given G, the com-putational graph of a Transformer, we instead hypothesize that efficient adaptation can be achieved by updating only an existing subgraph C ⊆ G important for processing Dtgt ;Hereafter, we use “circuit” to denote the subgraph recovered by attribution patching and selected for adaptation. 

Circuit Fine-Tuning (CFT). We operationalize our hy-pothesis in two phases. (1) A distribution-aware circuit dis-covery phase identifies an adaptation circuit with respect to 

Dtgt , selecting the pretrained subgraph whose weights will be updated while all other modules stay frozen. Unlike sensi-tivity methods, which ask which parameters most influence arXiv:2608.08336v1 [cs.CV] 8 Aug 2026 the loss through their gradients, CFT asks which subgraph is most important for processing the target distribution under attribution. (2) A sparse adaptation phase then fine-tunes only the modules in the discovered subgraph, updating pre-trained weights already selected as important for the target distribution while all other modules stay frozen. 

Contributions. 

1. Compute-Efficient Fine-Tuning (CEFT). We show that parameter count, the dominant PEFT efficiency metric, is decoupled from training compute—both analytically (Sec. 3.3), and empirically (Fig. 2). We advocate compute-to-accuracy —cumulative training FLOPs and wall-clock time to reach a target accuracy—as an additional primary efficiency metric for transformer adaptation. 2. Circuit Fine-Tuning (CFT). We introduce Circuit Fine-Tuning (CFT), a compute-efficient method that converges in far fewer epochs than PEFT baselines, without added parameters or architectural change. Whereas circuit dis-covery is conventionally applied to a trained model to explain existing behavior, we formulate it against a near-zero-initialized probe head to select modules before train-ing, isolating the response of the backbone to the tar-get distribution rather than the preferences of a partic-ular classifier (Sec. 3.1). Fine-tuning the recovered cir-cuit alone yields a strong compute–accuracy trade-off (Sec. 4.2). 3. Evaluation across diverse backbones and domains. 

Across four backbones (ViT-B/16, Swin-v2-B, DINOv2, Gemma-3-4B-IT), two benchmarks (VTAB-1k, CBIS-DDSM), and a vision-language study (Gemma-3 on CUB-200), CFT converges in ∼20 epochs vs. 44 –96 for 

2.3–6.6× fewer training FLOPs and up to 16 × less wall-clock time. On VTAB-1k it stays with the top PEFT meth-ods and reaches within 0.7 points of the strongest base-line, and under a fixed short budget it is the most accurate method on CBIS-DDSM and on the VLM study—while retaining its compute advantage. 

## 2 Related Work 

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning. As foundation models scale, PEFT methods aim to match FFT performance while updating minimal parameters. Additive methods insert new learnable modules: Adapters (Houlsby et al. 2019) add bot-tleneck MLPs, VPT (Jia et al. 2022) prepends learnable to-kens, and AdaptFormer (Chen et al. 2022) introduces par-allel adapter branches. Modulation methods avoid architec-tural changes: LoRA (Hu et al. 2022) learns low-rank weight updates, BitFit (Ben Zaken, Goldberg, and Ravfogel 2022) tunes only bias terms, SSF (Lian et al. 2022) learns per-feature scale-and-shift factors, and FacT (Jie and Deng 2023) tensorizes adapter weights. All apply adaptation uniformly 

across the model. The most closely related prior work is SPT (He et al. 2023), which uses gradient-squared sensitivity to identify important parameters, then inserts LoRA/Adapter modules at selected positions. GPS (Zhang et al. 2024) simi-larly selects via parameter-gradient signals; in contrast, CFT relies on an activation-level corrupted-vs-clean attribution that measures module responsiveness on target distribution. Prompt-evolution methods such as Pro-VPT (Shang et al. 2025) refine VPT-style prompts but remain additive and re-quire ∼100 -epoch schedules. More recent methods continue to push parameter efficiency: SNELL (Shen et al. 2024) expands sparse tuning under a low training-memory foot-print, token-coordinated prompt attention (Liu et al. 2025) strengthens visual prompting, selective tuning can even sur-pass full fine-tuning (Yin et al. 2025), and correlated low-rank adaptation extends LoRA to ConvNets (Ran et al. 2025). These works optimize parameter budgets. Closest in motiva-tion are Ladder Side-Tuning (Sung, Cho, and Bansal 2022), which targets training memory and time rather than parame-ter count, and Surgical Fine-Tuning (Lee et al. 2023), which selects which blocks to tune according to the type of dis-tribution shift. Both share our premise that parameter count is not the operative cost; we differ in selecting modules by causal attribution before training, and in measuring compute-to-accuracy directly. 

Mechanistic interpretability and circuits. Mechanis-tic interpretability seeks to understand how neural net-works arrive at their decisions. Olah et al. (2020) identi-fied “circuits”—sparse subgraphs that implement specific functions. Elhage et al. (2021) formalized Transformer atten-tion heads as information-routing primitives. Early discovery methods like ACDC (Conmy et al. 2023) used expensive ac-tivation patching. Edge Attribution Patching (EAP) (Syed, Rager, and Conmy 2024) approximates patching via first-order gradients, and EAP-IG (Hanna, Pezzelle, and Belinkov 2024) incorporates integrated gradients (Sundararajan, Taly, and Yan 2017) to address gradient saturation. These tools have primarily been used for explanation ; we operationalize circuit-discovery attribution as a pre-fine-tuning selector for compute-efficient fine-tuning.

Inference cost and deployment. CFT modifies only the values of existing weight tensors. The architecture, tensor shapes, sequence length, and computational graph are iden-tical to the original ViT. This guarantees: (1) 1× infer-ence latency : no additional tokens (VPT), no extra layers (AdaptFormer), no merge step (SSF/SPT at deployment); (2) 

full kernel compatibility : FlashAttention (Dao et al. 2022), xFormers, TensorRT, and ONNX export work without mod-ification; and (3) drop-in deployment : the adapted model is a single .pth file loadable by any standard ViT implemen-tation. 

## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Datasets. VTAB-1k (Zhai et al. 2019): 19 visual classifica-tion tasks (Natural, Specialized, Structured), each with 1,000 training examples. CBIS-DDSM (Sawyer-Lee et al. 2016): mammography classification, which has a large domain shift from ImageNet. We additionally evaluate on FGVC (five fine-grained datasets —CUB-200-2011 (Wah et al. 2011), NABirds (Van Horn et al. 2015), Oxford Flowers (Nilsback and Zisserman 2008), Stanford Dogs (Khosla et al. 2011), Stanford Cars (Krause et al. 2013)); results are in the supple-mentary. 

Architectures. Our main backbone is ViT-B/16 (85.8M parameters, ImageNet-21k). We also test Swin-v2-B (88M), DINOv2 ViT-B/14 (86.6M), and Gemma-3-4B-IT (Kamath, Ferret et al. 2025). 

Baselines. Full Fine-Tuning (FFT),SSF (Lian et al. 2022), SPT (He et al. 2023), GPS (Zhang et al. 2024), Pro-VPT (Shang et al. 2025) and LoRA (Hu et al. 2022) on ViT-B/16, and AdaptFormer (Chen et al. 2022) on the Swin and DINOv2 backbones. We run each baseline with its of-ficial public code and default hyperparameters, except the learning rate, which we tune as described below, and report the mean over 3 seeds. 

CFT configuration. N =32 , S=12 , σ=10 −5; AdamW with a cosine schedule and no warmup; about 20 epochs on average. The budget k is chosen per task from [8% , 17%] on the same held-out validation split used for baseline learning-rate selection, and its cost is included in all reported compute. The supplementary shows accuracy varies little across this range. 

4.2 Results on VTAB-1k 

We evaluate three questions. First, how much accuracy does each method yield per unit of compute? This is our main result. Second, does building each baseline a recipe for a short schedule close the gap? Third, does an accuracy gap remain under very high compute? 

CFT reaches high accuracy with far less compute (Fig. 2).

4.4 Domain Shift 2: Vision-Language Model 

As a feasibility study, we apply CFT to Gemma-3-4B-IT on CUB-200 (Table 9). With a 5-epoch budget, CFT reaches 71.3% top-1 accuracy by training only the discovered circuit, which is 17% of the backbone parameters. It outperforms both LoRA, which updates a far smaller fraction ( ∼0.5% ), and full fine-tuning. This is a claim about placement , not parameter efficiency. At a fixed short budget, concentrat-ing updates on attribution-selected modules outperforms dis-tributing low-rank updates uniformly across layers, and the remaining 83% of parameters updated by full fine-tuning yield no benefit within this budget. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced Circuit Fine-Tuning (CFT), which selects the backbone subset to adapt by circuit attribution—using integrated-gradient edge attribution against a near-zero-initialized probe head to recover the circuit the backbone uses for the target distribution—rather than by loss-gradient sensitivity. Across VTAB-1k, a mammography benchmark, and four backbones, CFT reaches accuracy within 0.7 points of the strongest baseline at 2.3–6.6× fewer training FLOPs and up to 16 × less wall-clock time, with no learning-rate warmup and no added parameters.
